THE PROBLEM

How the old model drinks water

  • Evaporation is the cooling. Towers reject heat by boiling off water; most of what is withdrawn never comes back.
  • ~18,000 gal / MW / day for a traditional evaporative facility — roughly 2.6–6.6 million gallons per MW per year depending on climate and design.
  • Industry average WUE: 1.8–1.9 L/kWh, versus near zero for air-cooled or sealed-loop sites.
  • U.S. scale: 5.6B gal (2014) → 17B gal (2023). Hyperscale alone could reach 16–33B gal/yr by 2028 (LBNL).
  • Blowdown carries concentrated minerals and treatment chemicals to the sewer.

FOUR DRIVERS

Why the shift is happening now

▲

Heat density

AI racks run 100–200 kW. NVIDIA Blackwell-class GPUs draw ~1,000–1,200 W each; Rubin racks are 100% liquid cooled with no fans. Air cannot carry that heat, and evaporative towers add cost without solving it at the chip.

●

Water stress & community pushback

Communities from Arizona to Georgia to Wisconsin now ask how many gallons a campus will take before zoning votes. Water has become a permitting issue, not just an operating cost.

■

Disclosure & regulation

The EU Energy Efficiency Directive requires data centers to report water use. Google (June 2026) published proposed industry water standards. WUE now sits beside PUE in procurement and ESG reporting.

◆

Warm water changes the math

Chips tolerate 45°C coolant. Heat at that temperature can be dumped to outdoor air nearly year-round — no tower, no chiller, and a smaller electric bill.

THE METRIC

Measuring it: Water Usage Effectiveness

WUE = site water (L) ÷ IT energy (kWh)
  • The water counterpart to PUE. Lower is better; zero means no ongoing on-site consumption.
  • AWS reached 0.12 L/kWh in 2025 — down 52% from 2021 — alongside a PUE of 1.14.
  • Microsoft improved 39% from 2021 to FY2024 and says its new designs target near-zero.
  • WUE counts on-site water only. Water used at power plants (“source” or indirect water) is extra — one reason on-site generation choices matter.

HEAT REJECTION

Method 1 — Dry coolers & adiabatic hybrids

  • How it works: fans push outdoor air across a finned coil — a car radiator at building scale. Water stays inside the pipe.
  • Adiabatic assist: on the hottest hours, pads pre-cool inlet air with a small spray. Water is used only when needed, a fraction of a tower's annual draw.
  • The limit: a dry cooler can only get the loop a few degrees above outdoor dry-bulb. With 45°C chip coolant, that ceiling rarely matters.
  • Tradeoff: more fan energy and more roof or yard area than a tower; noise must be managed.

THE MICROSOFT MODEL

Method 2 — Closed-loop chilled water, filled once

  • Sealed loop: water is charged at construction and circulates between servers and chillers indefinitely. Small makeup volumes cover leaks, service and flushing.
  • Economizing chillers: high-efficiency air-cooled chillers (often oil-free, magnetic-bearing compressors) run only when outdoor air can't do the job alone.
  • Elevated water temperatures widen the free-cooling window and keep the energy penalty “nominal,” per Microsoft.
  • Result: 125M+ liters avoided per data center per year; Fairwater's annual draw compared to a single restaurant (company claim).

THE KEY UNLOCK

Method 3 — Direct-to-chip liquid cooling (warm water)

  • Cold plates sit directly on GPUs and CPUs; liquid carries ~70–100% of rack heat, air handles the rest.
  • Warm-water design: ASHRAE liquid classes W32–W45 and NVIDIA's 45°C spec mean the return is hot enough to dump to outdoor air almost all year.
  • Raising plant water temperature 1°C cuts cooling cost ~4% (NVIDIA).
  • AWS IRHX retrofits this into existing air-cooled halls without new construction.
  • Single-phase cold plates top out near 1.5–2 kW per chip — the next slides show what comes after.

SUBMERGE THE SERVER

Method 4 — Immersion cooling

  • Single-phase: servers sit in a bath of non-conductive oil-like fluid that is pumped to a heat exchanger.
  • Two-phase: an engineered fluid boils on the chip surface and condenses on a coil above — very high heat transfer.
  • Efficiency: vendor-reported PUE as low as ~1.03–1.08, versus a global industry average of 1.56 (Uptime Institute, 2024); no server fans.
  • Water: none at the rack; heat leaves through a dry cooler.
  • Watch-outs: heavier floors, service handling, hardware warranties, and fluid chemistry — many two-phase fluids are fluorinated (PFAS / greenhouse-gas concerns).

CLOSING THE LOOP

Method 6 — Water sourcing & heat reuse

Reclaimed water

Treated municipal wastewater replaces potable makeup. AWS: 26 sites on reclaimed water in 2025, agreements with 13 utilities covering 130 data centers. EPA's Water Reuse Action Plan supports industrial cooling reuse.

On-site treatment & recovery

High-recovery treatment (e.g., Gradiant-type systems) cycles water many more times and shrinks blowdown. Rainwater harvesting covers makeup at some sites (AWS: 14 data centers).

Heat reuse

45–55°C return water is useful heat: district heating, greenhouses, and industrial process loads. Where on-site generation exists, turbine exhaust can drive absorption chillers — cooling from waste heat, not electricity.

The trend: water becomes a backup input sourced from wastewater, and heat becomes a product instead of a waste stream.

WHAT IT COSTS

The honest tradeoff: water vs. energy

  • Evaporation is thermodynamically cheap. Replacing it with fans and chillers costs electricity: Google puts air cooling at ~10% more energy on average and roughly double on hot days.
  • Microsoft reports only a nominal annual increase by running water warmer and economizing.
  • Warm-water liquid cooling flips the equation — it removes both the tower and most chiller hours.
  • Power plants use water too. A water-free data center on a water-cooled grid still has an indirect footprint.
WHERE IT WORKS BEST

Climates where outdoor air stays cool most of the year

The colder the air, the more hours a dry cooler handles alone. That makes the Great Lakes region one of the lowest-penalty places in the U.S. to build water-free AI capacity.

CLEVELAND APPLICATION

Applying it in Cleveland: a water-neutral stack

Climate

Cold winters and moderate summers keep outdoor air below 45°C coolant temperatures year-round, so warm-water DLC can reject heat with dry coolers alone for most hours.

Water

Lake Erie proximity plus NEORSD treated effluent as a reclaimed-water backup for makeup, flushing and emergencies — no potable draw, no blowdown.

Industry

Prefabricated mechanical floors, CDU skids, risers and roof dry-cooler platforms are manufacturable locally — fits the HOLON / B-Core factory strategy.

Result: zero evaporative loss and zero potable-water demand for cooling.

WHAT THIS MEANS

Takeaways

For the industry

  • Evaporative towers are being designed out of new AI capacity.
  • Warm-water direct-to-chip + dry coolers is the new baseline; immersion, two-phase and microfluidics follow as chips get hotter.
  • Water becomes a backup input from reclaimed sources; heat becomes a product.

For Cleveland

  • Climate makes water-free cooling cheaper here than in the Sun Belt.
  • A zero-evaporation commitment answers the first question every neighborhood will ask.
  • The mechanical stack — CDUs, manifolds, dry coolers, prefabricated floors — is a local manufacturing opportunity.
  • Next step: add taxes, equipment refresh and lease-up to the pro forma before investor use.
